When does the mean beat the median?

One unusual value can pull an average away from a known target. But the median is not automatically the better answer. Make a prediction, check two opposite examples, then change the numbers yourself.

Try your own numbers ↗See the two examples ↓

These are invented teaching examples with a target chosen for each case. In real work, the target may be unknown. An unusual observation is not automatically wrong, and comparing errors here does not prove a universal rule.

Predict before you reveal

The same two estimators. Opposite outcomes.

Case 1: one high reading

48 · 49 · 50 · 51 · 85

Known target: 50. Which estimate lands closer?

Reveal the arithmetic

Mean = 283 ÷ 5 = 56.6 (error 6.6). Median = 50 (error 0). The median is closer to this target. Remove 85 and the mean becomes 49.5; the median also becomes 49.5.

Case 2: the mean wins

40 · 42 · 50 · 60 · 68

Known target: 52. Which estimate lands closer?

Reveal the arithmetic

Mean = 260 ÷ 5 = 52 (error 0). Median = 50 (error 2). The mean is closer to this target. The target is supplied for the exercise, not inferred from the observations.

Teach it or explore it

A five-minute activity

  1. Predict: read each set of five observations and choose mean or median before opening the answer, or try a blind shareable challenge.
  2. Test: open the workbench. Use the two example buttons, then paste a set of your own 3–40 numbers.
  3. Challenge: remove the value farthest from the original median. Which estimate changes more? The workbench computes both changes.
  4. Extend: open the outcome atlas. At fifth reading 50, why can the mean have lower average error even when the median wins more individual cases? Select a cell, then open it in the full lab.

The generated cohort is deterministic toy data, not a sample of real observers. The workbench keeps your own numbers on your device and offers a local JSON download.

Questions people ask

Is the median always more accurate when there is an outlier?

No. Accuracy depends on a reference target and how the observations were produced. The second example shows a mean with zero error against its stated target.

What if I do not know the true value?

You can still compare mean, median, and how much each moves when one observation is removed. Without a trustworthy target, this lab does not call either estimate more accurate.

Should I delete a value because it is far from the median?

No. The removal is a sensitivity test. Keep or exclude an observation based on your data collection and analysis rules, not on this chart alone.
